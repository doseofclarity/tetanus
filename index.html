Communicable Diseases

Tetanus
(Lockjaw)

"The Silent Spore. The Deadly Spasm."


Agent & Transmission

The Agent

  • Pathogen: Clostridium tetani
  • Characteristics: Gram-positive, spore-forming bacillus. It is strictly Anaerobic (thrives in environments without oxygen).
  • The Toxin: The bacteria itself doesn't cause the spasms; it releases a deadly neurotoxin called Tetanospasmin.
  • Incubation Period: Usually 3 to 21 days (average 8 days). The further the wound is from the CNS, the longer the incubation.

Transmission Dynamics

Tetanus is NOT transmitted from person to person. It is acquired through environmental exposure.

Environmental Spores

Spores are ubiquitous in soil, dust, and animal/human feces. They are highly resilient and can survive for years.

Mode of Entry

Spores enter the body through breaks in the skin, especially deep puncture wounds, burns, crush injuries, or unhygienic cutting of the umbilical cord (Neonatal Tetanus).

Clinical Manifestations

Tetanospasmin blocks inhibitory neurotransmitters in the central nervous system, leading to sustained, severe muscle contractions (spasms) that progress in a descending pattern (jaw → face → neck → trunk → extremities).

1. Trismus

"Lockjaw"

Usually the first sign. Spasm of the masseter muscles makes it difficult or impossible for the patient to open their mouth or chew.

2. Risus Sardonicus

"Sardonic Smile"

Sustained spasm of the facial muscles produces a grotesque, fixed, grinning expression with raised eyebrows.

3. Opisthotonos

Severe Back Arching

A profound, rigid spasm of the extensor muscles of the neck and back. The body arches backward so intensely that only the head and heels touch the bed.

The Nature of the Spasms

The muscle spasms are excruciatingly painful. Crucially, they are triggered by sensory stimuli. Minor disturbances—such as a sudden noise, bright light, a draft of air, or even light touch—can precipitate generalized, violent convulsions. The patient remains fully conscious during these spasms.

The Classic Triad

T. R. O.

(Hover or Tap to Flip)

Trismus (Lockjaw)
Risus Sardonicus (Grin)
Opisthotonos (Back arch)

Tetanus Complications

Without medical intervention, the fatality rate of generalized tetanus is incredibly high. Complications arise primarily from the physical force of the spasms and the neurotoxin's effect on the nervous system.

Leading Cause of Death

Respiratory Failure

Spasms can affect the vocal cords (laryngospasm) and the respiratory muscles (diaphragm and intercostals), leading to asphyxiation. Aspiration pneumonia is also highly common due to inability to swallow.

Bone Fractures

The muscle contractions are so violent that they can literally tear muscles and cause compression fractures of the spine and long bones.

Autonomic Dysfunction

In severe cases, the neurotoxin affects the autonomic nervous system causing extreme fluctuations in blood pressure, profuse sweating, and cardiac arrhythmias.

Prevention & Nursing Care

Maternal Tetanus Toxoid (TT) Schedule

A critical PHN role is immunizing pregnant women to prevent Maternal and Neonatal Tetanus. The vaccine creates antibodies that cross the placenta.

Dose When to give % Protection & Duration
TT 1 As early as possible in pregnancy No protection yet
TT 2 At least 4 weeks after TT 1 80% protection (lasts 3 years)
TT 3 At least 6 months after TT 2 95% protection (lasts 5 years)
TT 4 At least 1 year after TT 3 99% protection (lasts 10 years)
TT 5 At least 1 year after TT 4 99% protection (lasts Lifetime)

Clinical Nursing Management

1. Environmental Control (Crucial Priority)

Because spasms are triggered by sensory stimuli, the patient must be placed in a dark, completely quiet room. Group nursing interventions to minimize touching and disturbing the patient.

2. Medical Countermeasures

  • Neutralize Toxin: Administer Tetanus Immune Globulin (TIG) or Antitoxin. (Note: This only neutralizes *unbound* toxin circulating in blood; it cannot unbind toxin already attached to nerve endings).
  • Antibiotics: Metronidazole or Penicillin to destroy the *C. tetani* bacteria and stop further toxin production.
  • Muscle Relaxants: Diazepam (Valium) or other sedatives to control severe spasms and induce relaxation.

3. Airway Management

Maintain a patent airway. Keep an emergency tracheostomy tray and suction equipment readily available at the bedside due to the high risk of laryngospasm and respiratory failure.
